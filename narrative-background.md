# Space Trader - Narrative Background

Last updated: April 1, 2026

## Purpose
This document captures the current narrative framing for `Space Trader` based on the live factions, map, and mechanics in the prototype.

It is intended to answer two questions:
- why these battles are happening
- how the card-game and tactics mechanics map onto the reality of the setting

## The Setting: The Frontier Belt
The game takes place on the `Frontier Belt`, a chain of mineral-rich planets and planetoids on the edge of known space. These worlds are not home to major civilizations, but they are heavily contested free-trade zones where the laws of the core systems barely apply.

Every operation in the Belt is temporary, extractive, and dangerous. Factions land only what they need, secure resource nodes as quickly as possible, and try to force their rivals into a `Market Exit` before the campaign becomes unprofitable.

On the battlefield, each commander fights from a surface base. In fiction, those bases are local processing hubs, relay stations, and fabrication anchors. On the strategic level, the real operation extends upward into orbit.

## The Core Narrative: The Orbital Pipeline
The battle on the ground is only half the story. Each faction commander is supported by a large allied satellite in high orbit that stores blueprints, tactical payloads, and deployment authority for the entire expedition.

### Cards As Data Packets
In-setting, drawing a card is a `satellite download`.

Your deck represents a queued library of pre-purchased blueprints, tactical protocols, and unit authorizations stored in orbital memory. A card in hand is not a physical object. It is an available packet of matter, software, or command authority that can now be transmitted to the surface.

### The Stack As Signal Priority
When a card is played, the commander is not "casting a spell" in the traditional fantasy sense. They are pushing a transmission through a crowded combat theater.

The `Stack` represents contested signal priority and local bandwidth limits:
- slower transmissions take time to resolve into surface reality
- faster responses can interrupt or supersede those transmissions
- a `Counter Pulse` is effectively a signal jammer or packet corrupter that prevents a hostile instruction from resolving cleanly

### Energy And Material Constraints
Deploying units and battlefield effects is expensive because orbital fabrication still requires physical feedstock and transmission energy.

That is why commanders need:
- `Credits` as a universal currency and logistics medium
- faction-specific materials such as `Alloy`, `Flux`, or `Biomass` as the print-medium required to materialize advanced assets

The economy is not abstract. It is the supply chain that makes orbital intervention possible.

## The Three Trading Houses

### Alloy Clan
The `Alloy Clan` is a coalition of mining guilds, foundries, and heavy-industry combines. They do not see the Frontier Belt as an ecosystem or a frontier to understand. They see it as a quarry to be stripped, processed, and shipped.

Their battlefield presence reflects that philosophy:
- heavy armored units
- rigid battlefield discipline
- industrial protocols built around attrition and salvage

`Salvage` is central to their identity. Destroyed enemy machinery is not waste. It is feedstock. The Clan folds wreckage back into the pipeline and turns battlefield destruction into more ore and more pressure.

Their tactical language is built around hardened doctrine:
- `War Protocol`
- `Scorched Protocol`
- `Bastion` formations that interlock defenses and reward stable front lines

The Alloy Clan wins by creating inevitability through infrastructure, discipline, and material superiority.

### Flux Collective
The `Flux Collective` is an opaque network of data-traders, signal architects, and energy-focused operators. They are less interested in mining the Belt than in exploiting its unstable fields, transmission corridors, and ambient power anomalies.

Their battlefield identity is built around precision and connectivity rather than mass:
- relay-linked units that extend orbital reach
- tempo-driven tactics
- rapid signal chaining and response windows

`Relay` is both their fiction and their mechanical identity. Their units form a living communication lattice, allowing the Collective to project influence through positioning instead of raw durability.

They also specialize in `Surge` behavior: overclocked uplinks and amplified tactics that become more powerful when multiple signals are pushed through the theater in quick succession. The Flux Collective trades in time, sequencing, and tempo rather than brute force.

### Biomass Swarm
The `Biomass Swarm` is not a single species or state. It is a loose but coordinated ecology of organic traders, engineered organisms, and growth-based expeditionary systems. Where other factions deploy infrastructure, the Swarm cultivates it.

They view the planet as substrate for expansion:
- units are seeded rather than manufactured
- support systems are grown rather than assembled
- battlefield momentum comes from multiplication and adaptation

`Sprout` represents drop-pod organisms or fast-acclimating bioforms that can act immediately after deployment. `Bloom` represents metabolic chain reactions, where enhancement and stimulation trigger additional resource release. What looks like a buff in gameplay is, in fiction, a surge of growth and biological productivity.

The Biomass Swarm wins by turning the battlefield itself into a living engine.

## Story Translation For Core Mechanics

### Resource Nodes
Resource nodes are not generic mana points. They are strategically valuable extraction sites and ancient infrastructure remnants scattered across the Belt:
- `Trade Beacons` for credits and commercial routing
- `Ore Mines` for alloy extraction
- `Ion Vents` for flux capture
- `Xenobogs` for harvestable biomass

Controlling a node means establishing enough local dominance to route extraction through your own operation. Harvesters then collect the material and return it to the base's local processing network, where it can be fed back into orbital fabrication.

### Harvesters
Resource units are expedition specialists: drones, crawlers, tenders, or bio-haulers designed to move cargo through an unstable warzone.

Mechanically, they gather from controlled nodes and return cargo to base-adjacent dropoff tiles. In fiction, they are the last-mile logistics layer between raw extraction and orbital deployment.

### Summoning Sickness
Summoning sickness is best understood as `deployment calibration`.

When a unit is first transmitted or fabricated onto the surface, it still needs time to sync with local gravity, terrain, atmosphere, targeting data, and command relays. Most units cannot act immediately because they have not fully stabilized in theater.

`Sprout` bypasses that delay by representing systems intentionally designed for immediate field activation.

### Bases
A base is not just a health bar. It is the local anchor for:
- orbital uplink
- fabrication routing
- cargo processing
- battlefield command

Destroying a base means collapsing the expedition's ability to sustain operations on that world.

### Victory
You do not simply "win a match." You force a `Market Exit`.

By destroying the enemy base, you eliminate their local relay and processing capacity, making the campaign too expensive and too unstable to continue. Their satellite may still exist in orbit, but without a viable surface anchor, the trading house loses the planet.

## Current Tone
The setting should feel like a mix of:
- frontier extraction economy
- orbital logistics war
- commercial rivalry pushed into open battlefield violence

These are not noble empires fighting for destiny. They are competing trading powers trying to make a hostile world pay for itself before somebody else burns their operation down.
